What does it take to reason seriously about energy infrastructure beyond Earth?

Not simply to calculate whether one technology can produce power, but to determine whether an entire energy architecture can support a mission when mass, storage, reliability, logistics, maintenance, environmental conditions, uncertainty, scientific evidence, and operational constraints are considered together.

Harm Booij, an independent software developer and researcher working as TheArchitect max, discusses the thinking behind the FusionLunar Energy Systems Engineering Platform: a proprietary, evidence-governed engineering and research platform being developed to evaluate energy architectures for lunar and cislunar applications.

The project does not begin with the assumption that fusion, solar, nuclear fission, beamed power, or any other technology must win.

Its purpose is more demanding:

to determine which architecture can be defended under a defined mission, which cannot, how uncertain that conclusion is, and what evidence would be required to change it.

Who

The Creator and the Engineering Philosophy

Who is Harm Booij, and what led you to develop FusionLunar?

I am an independent software developer and researcher working under the identity TheArchitect max.

My interest in this project began with a broader engineering problem.

When people discuss future lunar infrastructure, advanced propulsion, fusion energy, helium-3, surface power, or cislunar industry, individual technologies are often discussed in isolation.

That can be useful for scientific study, but an actual infrastructure decision cannot be made in isolation.

An energy technology must ultimately function within a system.

It has to satisfy a mission. It has to survive an environment. It has to interact with storage, power distribution, logistics, deployment, maintenance, redundancy, available resources, operational requirements, and uncertainty.

I became interested in whether those questions could be brought together within one disciplined engineering environment.

That became FusionLunar.

Are you primarily approaching the project as a fusion researcher?

No.

Fusion is an important research domain within the project, but FusionLunar should not be understood as software whose purpose is to demonstrate that fusion is preferable.

That would compromise the decision process before the analysis had even begun.

My objective is technology-neutral engineering.

If a solar-and-storage architecture is the strongest solution for a particular mission, the platform should be capable of saying so.

If fission performs better under another set of constraints, that should be visible.

If a hybrid architecture offers the most resilient trade-off, the system should be capable of identifying that.

And if a proposed fusion architecture does not have sufficient evidence, energy balance, resource support, or engineering maturity, then the appropriate result may simply be:

insufficient evidence.

That is not a disappointing output.

It is often a more valuable engineering conclusion than an unjustifiably precise answer.

Who is FusionLunar ultimately intended to serve?

The long-term audience is broader than one scientific discipline.

Potential users include energy-system engineers, mission designers, researchers, advanced-technology teams, aerospace organizations, infrastructure planners, system architects, technical decision-makers, and organizations evaluating future lunar or cislunar capabilities.

Different users may approach the same problem from very different directions.

A physicist may be concerned with the credibility of an energy model.

A mission architect may care about continuous power and landed mass.

A logistics specialist may care about replacement burden and resupply.

A systems engineer may be concerned with interfaces, failure modes, redundancy, and availability.

A decision-maker may need to understand why one architecture was preferred over another.

FusionLunar is intended to connect those perspectives without pretending that they are identical.

What

Defining FusionLunar

What exactly is the FusionLunar Energy Systems Engineering Platform?

FusionLunar is a proprietary engineering and research platform for the design, simulation, qualification, comparison, and audit of lunar and cislunar energy-system architectures.

The fundamental idea is that competing energy technologies should be examined under a common mission and engineering context.

That may include architectures based on solar generation and storage, fission, hybrid systems, orbital or beamed power, future fusion concepts, and other technologies that can satisfy a common engineering interface.

The objective is not merely to calculate how much power something produces.

The objective is to understand the consequences of choosing that architecture.

How much mass must be delivered?

Can continuous power be maintained?

How much storage is required?

What happens during extended low-generation periods?

What are the logistics implications?

How dependent is the architecture on local resources?

How much redundancy is required?

How sensitive is the result to uncertain assumptions?

How mature is the evidence supporting the conclusion?

Those questions are much closer to real engineering decisions.

So it is more than a simulation tool?

That is the intention.

Simulation is necessary, but simulation alone is not sufficient.

A simulation can produce a highly precise numerical result based on weak assumptions.

It can produce a detailed answer using uncertain inputs.

It can even be internally consistent while remaining inappropriate for the real system being studied.

That is why FusionLunar is being developed around a broader chain of reasoning.

At a high level, I think of that chain as:

mission requirements → candidate architectures → evidence → models → simulation → uncertainty → qualification → comparison → engineering decision

Every stage matters.

The final number is not the entire result.

The reasoning that produced it is part of the result.

What types of energy architectures should eventually be comparable within the platform?

The system is intended to remain technology-neutral.

Examples include lunar surface solar with energy storage, regenerative storage systems, nuclear fission, solar-fission hybrids, orbital or beamed-power concepts, and future fusion architectures.

D–He3 fusion is relevant because of its historical association with lunar resource concepts, but it does not receive privileged status.

Other technologies can also be introduced where their engineering assumptions and evidence can be represented appropriately.

The important principle is that different architectures should be compared under consistent mission constraints rather than under selectively favorable assumptions.

What does “evidence-governed” mean in this context?

It means that important engineering claims should not float independently from their basis.

A result should be traceable to the assumptions, data, model domain, uncertainty, and qualification status that support it.

For example, there is an important difference between a measured quantity, a value reproduced from a published source, a simulated result, a theoretical estimate, and a hypothesis.

Those categories should not silently collapse into one another.

The same applies to missing information.

Unknown should remain unknown.

It should not quietly become zero merely because zero is computationally convenient.

I consider that distinction essential when dealing with ambitious technologies.

Why

The Fundamental Problem

Why does FusionLunar need to exist?

Because future off-world energy decisions are systems problems, but discussions about them are often technology problems.

Those are not the same thing.

A reactor may have attractive power density, but the complete architecture may introduce deployment, shielding, thermal, maintenance, logistics, or qualification consequences.

A solar architecture may appear straightforward until energy storage, location, operating continuity, degradation, redundancy, and lunar-night requirements are included.

A fusion concept may appear attractive at the level of reaction physics while remaining unresolved at the level of complete power balance, fuel supply, conversion systems, maintainability, mass, or experimental evidence.

A system-level platform forces those interactions into the same conversation.

That is the reason for FusionLunar.

Why not simply use separate specialist tools for each technology?

Specialist tools are important and in many cases indispensable.

FusionLunar is not intended to replace specialist scientific software.

The problem arises when the output of several specialist domains must become one architecture decision.

Different tools may use different assumptions, maturity standards, uncertainty conventions, and system boundaries.

One model may optimize a physical subsystem while another assumes an ideal interface around it.

Those local optimizations do not automatically produce a globally viable system.

FusionLunar is intended to provide the systems-engineering layer in which those contributions can be interpreted under a common decision framework.

Why is technology neutrality so important?

Because otherwise the software becomes an argument disguised as an engineering tool.

If the purpose of the system is to prove that a particular technology wins, then assumptions can gradually be selected in ways that support that result.

That may happen deliberately or unconsciously.

I want the platform to resist that tendency.

A credible engineering environment should be capable of disproving the hypothesis that motivated the investigation.

Fusion should be allowed to lose.

Solar should be allowed to lose.

Fission should be allowed to lose.

A hybrid should be allowed to lose.

And sometimes every current option may remain inadequately supported.

That intellectual independence is fundamental to the project.

Why place so much emphasis on uncertainty?

Because a deterministic number can create an illusion of certainty.

Future lunar infrastructure inevitably involves uncertain parameters.

Some uncertainty arises from physical measurements.

Some comes from environmental variability.

Some comes from incomplete engineering definition.

Some comes from resource estimates, lifetime assumptions, maintenance models, logistics, failure rates, technology maturity, or extrapolation beyond validated conditions.

If the architecture ranking changes significantly when a plausible parameter changes, that matters.

The decision-maker should know that.

A platform that only reports nominal performance can hide the most important characteristic of the decision: its fragility.

Where

Lunar and Cislunar Applications

Where do you see FusionLunar being most useful?

Its strongest application is where multiple energy architectures must be considered for a defined lunar or cislunar mission.

That could involve a surface installation, a science outpost, industrial infrastructure, resource-processing activities, logistics nodes, or more complex future infrastructure.

The exact mission can vary.

What matters is that energy is not evaluated as an isolated generator.

It is evaluated as part of the mission architecture.

Why include cislunar systems rather than focusing only on the lunar surface?

Because future lunar infrastructure is unlikely to exist as an isolated surface problem.

Transportation, orbital infrastructure, communication, logistics, resource movement, power transfer, servicing, and operational dependencies can extend beyond the surface.

An energy architecture may therefore have consequences across a larger Earth-Moon operational environment.

I want the platform to be capable of reasoning at that systems level.

That does not mean every possible cislunar scenario is already implemented or qualified.

It means the product direction recognizes that surface energy and surrounding infrastructure are interconnected problems.

Could the platform eventually compare geographically different lunar sites?

That is a natural long-term application.

Location changes the engineering problem.

Illumination conditions, thermal environment, communication geometry, terrain, logistics, resource assumptions, and operational constraints may differ substantially between candidate sites.

An architecture that performs well under one environment should not automatically be assumed to perform equally well elsewhere.

The platform should ultimately make those dependencies explicit.

When

Development, Qualification, and Readiness

How far has the project progressed?

FusionLunar has moved well beyond an initial concept.

A substantial engineering and research software foundation has been developed, and the repository contains an established history of deterministic software validation, numerical credibility work, provenance controls, and release qualification.

The current qualified software baseline is version 0.46.

Development version 0.47 is undergoing an additional long-running numerical reproducibility qualification campaign.

That distinction is important.

Version 0.47 is not being presented as completed merely because large parts of the software already pass their regression and packaging checks.

Its terminal qualification work is still in progress.

Why is the current qualification campaign taking so much effort?

Because reproducibility should be demonstrated under realistic execution conditions rather than assumed.

When a numerical engineering platform becomes more complex, the question is not only whether individual functions work.

You also want confidence that substantial campaigns can be resumed, reproduced, compared, and audited without changing the meaning of the underlying engineering result.

Long-running computation creates practical problems as well.

Execution environments can stop.

Resources can be interrupted.

Work may need to continue across multiple sessions.

A serious engineering workflow needs a controlled way to preserve valid completed work while refusing to reuse anything whose identity or integrity can no longer be trusted.

The current qualification work is addressing that class of problem.

Does successful software qualification mean the physical energy technologies are validated?

Absolutely not.

This is one of the most important distinctions in the project.

Software qualification can establish properties of the software.

Numerical qualification can establish properties of numerical behaviour under defined conditions.

Neither automatically establishes that a physical technology works.

FusionLunar does not currently claim demonstrated D–He3 net power, commercial lunar helium-3 extraction, flight-qualified hardware, experimentally validated complete energy systems, certified mission reliability, or a demonstrated commercial Earth-Moon energy economy.

Those would require different forms of evidence.

I want the project to make those boundaries explicit rather than allowing one type of success to imply another.

When would you consider FusionLunar genuinely mature?

When its most important decisions can be supported by evidence appropriate to those decisions.

That requires several maturity layers.

The software itself must be reliable.

The numerical methods must be credible.

Scientific datasets must be traceable and appropriate to their domains.

Physical models must be compared with evidence.

Uncertainty must be represented rather than hidden.

Architecture comparisons must remain consistent.

The commercial product must also satisfy security, reproducibility, deployment, and operational requirements.

Eventually, where physical or hardware claims are made, those claims require experimental or hardware evidence.

Maturity is not one checkbox.

It is a chain.

How

Engineering Discipline and Evidence

How do you prevent ambitious research from turning into speculation?

By classifying what is actually known.

A hypothesis can be useful.

A theoretical result can be useful.

A simulation can be useful.

But they should remain what they are.

One of the project's underlying principles is that an attractive hypothesis does not gain maturity merely because it has been implemented in sophisticated software.

The software can help investigate the hypothesis.

It cannot manufacture evidence that does not exist.

How important are negative results?

Extremely important.

A serious engineering platform should be capable of telling the user that an architecture does not satisfy its requirements.

It should also be able to identify cases where the evidence is too weak to justify a conclusion.

That can be more useful than producing another optimized answer.

If a fusion concept fails a complete power balance, that matters.

If a resource assumption dominates the outcome but is poorly supported, that matters.

If a conventional architecture remains superior under the stated mission, that matters.

Engineering should expose those outcomes rather than protect the original idea.

What role does reproducibility play?

A central one.

If an important engineering conclusion cannot be reproduced from its defined inputs and assumptions, confidence in the result is limited.

Reproducibility also supports review.

Someone should be able to understand what was evaluated, under which assumptions, using which evidence state, and with which qualification status.

That does not mean every internal implementation detail must become public.

A proprietary platform can protect its intellectual property while still producing rigorous, auditable engineering outputs.

Those two objectives are compatible.

How do you think about provenance?

Provenance is the history of a result.

Where did the relevant information come from?

Which version was used?

Which assumptions were applied?

What transformation occurred?

What qualification state did the result have?

That history becomes increasingly important when a platform combines scientific data, system assumptions, simulation, uncertainty, and engineering decisions.

Without provenance, a sophisticated output can become difficult to interrogate.

With provenance, the decision becomes inspectable.

Does a cryptographically traceable artifact prove that the engineering is correct?

No.

It can prove important things about identity and integrity.

It can help establish that an artifact has not changed or that a particular result belongs to a particular evidence state.

But integrity is not the same as truth.

A perfectly authenticated incorrect assumption remains an incorrect assumption.

This is another distinction I want the platform to preserve.

Engineering confidence requires both integrity and validity.

How do you handle missing evidence?

Explicitly.

Missing evidence should not disappear into the model.

If an important parameter is unknown, that uncertainty should remain visible.

If a value requires extrapolation beyond available measurements, that should matter to the result.

If an architecture depends critically on a resource whose distribution is uncertain, that dependency should affect the decision.

The platform should prefer a defensible statement of uncertainty over a false appearance of completeness.

Fusion and lunar helium-3

The name FusionLunar naturally raises the question of lunar helium-3. How important is D–He3 to the project?

It is an important research pathway, but it is not the project's predetermined conclusion.

D–He3 fusion is interesting because it connects nuclear physics, plasma engineering, resource questions, lunar extraction, processing, logistics, power conversion, and infrastructure.

That makes it a particularly demanding systems problem.

But its conceptual appeal does not establish feasibility.

The relevant nuclear data, plasma conditions, complete power balance, lunar resource evidence, extraction requirements, system mass, conversion losses, recirculating power, logistics, and uncertainty all have to be examined.

The platform must remain capable of concluding that the evidence is insufficient or that another architecture is preferable.

Do you expect the Moon to become an important source of helium-3?

That is a question requiring evidence, not a premise I want to embed in the platform.

The existence of helium-3 in lunar regolith and the practical engineering case for industrial extraction are very different claims.

Resource concentration, geographic variation, excavation requirements, processing energy, throughput, equipment lifetime, logistics, and downstream fusion requirements all matter.

A resource can exist without being economically or energetically useful.

FusionLunar should help make that distinction explicit.

What would a credible fusion evaluation eventually require?

A complete chain.

Credible nuclear data.

Well-defined reaction and plasma models.

A realistic power balance.

Explicit auxiliary and recirculating power.

Conversion efficiency.

Thermal management.

Fuel and resource requirements.

System mass.

Reliability.

Maintenance.

Deployment.

Operational conditions.

Uncertainty.

And appropriate experimental evidence.

If one part of that chain is speculative, the final claim should retain that limitation.

Technology comparison

What makes architecture comparison difficult?

Technologies are often optimized around different strengths.

Solar may perform extremely well in one environment but require substantial storage or geographic adaptation in another.

Fission can provide continuous power but introduces its own engineering and deployment considerations.

Beamed power changes where generation and consumption occur but introduces transmission, pointing, infrastructure, and efficiency questions.

Fusion could offer significant long-term advantages if the necessary physics and engineering become supportable, but its current evidence maturity is fundamentally different from mature technologies.

A fair comparison cannot simply place one headline metric beside another.

The system boundary has to be consistent.

Which comparison dimensions matter most?

That depends on the mission, but important dimensions include continuous-power capability, landed mass, storage requirements, deployment burden, logistics, maintenance, redundancy, reliability, failure sensitivity, resource dependence, uncertainty, evidence maturity, and qualification status.

Cost may also become important, but economic outputs must have explicit boundaries and credible assumptions.

There is no universal single metric that determines the best architecture.

The preferred solution depends on the mission.

Does that mean FusionLunar will simply generate one score for every technology?

That would be too simplistic.

Reducing a complex engineering trade space to one opaque score can hide more than it reveals.

Different stakeholders may legitimately assign different importance to mass, resilience, cost, maintenance, technology maturity, or resource dependence.

I am more interested in transparent trade spaces.

A decision should be understandable.

The user should be able to see why an architecture performs well, where it performs poorly, and which assumptions dominate the result.

The decision platform

What is the eventual commercial product supposed to become?

The long-term direction is a professional engineering and decision platform.

A user should eventually be able to define a mission, configure candidate architectures, associate evidence and assumptions with those architectures, execute engineering studies, examine uncertainty and sensitivities, compare alternatives, and produce reproducible engineering decision packages.

The product should support individual technical analysis as well as more structured organizational workflows.

Future delivery models may include professional hosted access, APIs, enterprise environments, and private deployments where required.

The proprietary engineering core remains distinct from any public research or educational material.

Why not simply make the entire platform open source?

Transparency and open source are not the same thing.

A proprietary engineering platform can be transparent about its assumptions, evidence categories, model domains, validation status, and limitations without publishing its complete implementation.

Public science should remain public.

Physical laws do not become proprietary because they are implemented in software.

Public datasets remain subject to their own rights.

Published algorithms and standards remain external knowledge.

The proprietary value lies in how the complete engineering system is constructed, governed, qualified, operated, and turned into a usable decision environment.

I think that boundary can be maintained responsibly.

The North Star

What is the project's North Star?

The North Star is straightforward to state, even though it is difficult to achieve:

Build an evidence-governed, technology-neutral engineering and decision platform that can determine which lunar or cislunar energy architecture is technically supportable for a given mission, under explicit constraints, uncertainty, logistics, reliability, and evidence—and explain exactly why.

That is the direction against which I evaluate the project.

Not whether the software becomes larger.

Not whether it produces impressive visualizations.

Not whether a particular technology wins.

The question is whether it improves the quality and defensibility of the engineering decision.

Why is that the North Star rather than “make fusion viable”?

Because software cannot make a physical technology viable by declaration.

If fusion becomes a superior architecture under a credible set of future conditions, the platform should identify that.

But if the evidence says otherwise, the platform should identify that too.

The scientific and commercial value comes from trustworthy discrimination between alternatives.

If FusionLunar were designed to produce one preferred answer, it would lose much of its purpose.

What single question should the mature platform ultimately answer?

Something close to this:

Given this mission, this location, this power demand, this operational lifetime, these logistics constraints, this uncertainty, and the evidence currently available—which energy architecture is defensible, which is not, and what new evidence could change that decision?

That question contains much of the project's purpose.

Intellectual independence

How important is intellectual independence in FusionLunar?

It is fundamental.

An engineering project should be allowed to challenge the assumptions on which it was founded.

That includes my own assumptions.

If a study shows that a more conventional architecture is better than a sophisticated one, I do not consider that a failure of the research.

I consider it useful information.

Likewise, if an apparently attractive concept depends on uncertain resource assumptions or unrealistic operating conditions, the software should expose that weakness.

The goal is not to defend an idea.

The goal is to improve the decision.

How do you avoid confusing complexity with quality?

By requiring complexity to justify itself.

A more detailed model is not automatically a better model.

A larger simulation is not automatically a more credible simulation.

More parameters can create more uncertainty rather than less.

The appropriate level of complexity depends on the question being asked and the evidence available to support the model.

I want FusionLunar to become sophisticated where sophistication adds engineering value, not merely because additional complexity is technically possible.

Validation and responsible claims

What does validation mean within this project?

It depends on what is being validated.

Software validation is different from numerical qualification.

Numerical qualification is different from comparison with experimental measurements.

Experimental validation is different from hardware qualification.

Hardware qualification is different from operational certification.

Those terms should not be used interchangeably.

The repository deliberately separates different evidence classes because a successful software test does not establish that an external physical technology is ready for deployment.

Why emphasize that distinction so strongly?

Because advanced technology attracts exaggerated language very easily.

Words such as “validated,” “verified,” “qualified,” and “proven” can sound definitive even when they refer to a narrow test.

That can create false confidence.

I would rather state exactly what has been demonstrated and exactly what remains unresolved.

Credibility comes partly from refusing to claim more than the evidence supports.

Is FusionLunar already commercially ready?

No.

It is a developed proprietary engineering platform with substantial software and qualification work behind it, but the broader product roadmap extends significantly beyond the current release.

Scientific data qualification, technology-specific evidence, integrated architecture comparison, higher-fidelity system coupling, uncertainty and model-risk development, commercial runtime, security, deployment, and eventual whole-product qualification remain part of the planned development path.

The current software foundation is meaningful.

It should not be confused with completion of the entire commercial product.

Future vision

The ultimate objective

What is your ultimate ambition for FusionLunar?

My ultimate ambition is to create a serious engineering environment for reasoning about energy infrastructure beyond Earth.

I want a system in which advanced ideas can be investigated without receiving special treatment simply because they are ambitious.

I want established technologies to be challenged under the same conditions.

I want uncertainty to remain visible.

I want assumptions to remain traceable.

I want evidence quality to influence confidence.

I want negative results to remain acceptable.

And I want the final engineering decision to be reproducible and explainable.

If the platform eventually helps an engineer distinguish between an attractive concept and a defensible architecture, then it will be doing something valuable.

What would distinguish success from simply having a very sophisticated simulation platform?

Decision quality.

A sophisticated simulator may produce enormous quantities of data.

That does not automatically make the engineering decision better.

Success would mean that FusionLunar helps users understand the architecture trade space, identify dominant uncertainties, challenge weak assumptions, compare alternatives consistently, and determine what evidence is still required.

The platform should reduce unjustified confidence rather than manufacture it.

What would you like organizations using FusionLunar to trust?

Not that the software is always right.

That would be an irresponsible promise.

I would want them to trust the discipline of the process.

That the platform distinguishes evidence from assumptions.

That uncertainty is not silently removed.

That alternative architectures are not intentionally disadvantaged.

That the qualification state of a result is visible.

That important limitations remain visible.

And that a result can be revisited when better evidence becomes available.

That is a much more useful form of trust.

Do you see the project contributing to actual lunar infrastructure one day?

That is certainly an ambition.

But there is a large distance between developing engineering software and demonstrating that the software has influenced a successful operational system.

I do not want to compress those stages rhetorically.

The appropriate path is incremental.

Build the software foundation.

Qualify the numerical behaviour.

Strengthen the scientific evidence architecture.

Benchmark models.

Improve system coupling.

Evaluate technologies under common constraints.

Validate where real evidence becomes available.

Then, if the platform proves valuable in serious engineering environments, operational relevance can follow.

That sequence matters.

What is the broader significance of the project beyond lunar energy?

The broader idea is that ambitious engineering needs disciplined uncertainty.

Many future technologies are evaluated through combinations of theory, simulations, limited experiments, historical data, and assumptions about systems that do not yet exist.

That creates a strong temptation to collapse possibility into feasibility.

FusionLunar is an attempt to do the opposite.

It treats possibility as the beginning of an investigation.

Feasibility has to be earned through evidence.

That principle extends well beyond lunar energy.

If future evidence proves that some of your original assumptions were wrong, would that undermine the project?

No.

If the platform is working properly, it should be designed to accommodate that.

Scientific and engineering knowledge changes.

Measurements improve.

Technology matures.

Some concepts fail.

New architectures appear.

A useful decision platform must be capable of incorporating new evidence and revisiting previous conclusions.

A system that can only preserve its original conclusions is not an evidence-governed system.

Finally, if you had to summarize your vision for FusionLunar in one statement, what would you say?

“I want FusionLunar to make ambitious lunar and cislunar energy ideas answerable to engineering reality. The objective is not to prove that fusion, solar, fission, or any other technology must win. It is to build a disciplined environment in which mission requirements, scientific evidence, system constraints, uncertainty, and reproducible analysis can determine which architecture is actually defensible—and make clear when the evidence is not yet sufficient to decide.”
