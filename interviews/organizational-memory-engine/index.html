How can organizations transform years of software development, architectural decisions, incidents, and operational experience into a durable form of intelligence? Harm Booij, an independent software developer and researcher operating as TheArchitect max, discusses his vision for the Organizational Memory Engine (OME): a proprietary platform designed to preserve engineering knowledge, evaluate software changes, and make consequential technical decisions more informed, traceable, and accountable.

Who

The Creator and the People Behind the Decisions

Who is Harm Booij, and what is the philosophy behind TheArchitect max?

I am an independent software developer and researcher working under the identity TheArchitect max. My work is driven by a fundamental interest in designing systems that are not merely functional, but intellectually defensible, technically rigorous, and capable of solving problems that conventional software often leaves unresolved.

I do not believe that software development should be measured primarily by how much code is written, how many features are released, or how quickly a product reaches the market.

I believe it should be measured by the problems it solves, the quality of the evidence supporting its operation, and the degree to which its architecture remains reliable under real world conditions.

With the Organizational Memory Engine, I am pursuing something more substantial than another development productivity tool.

I want to address a structural weakness in how organizations learn from their own technical history.

Who is the Organizational Memory Engine ultimately intended for?

OME is intended for organizations where software decisions have consequences beyond the immediate development task.

That includes software engineering teams, technology companies, enterprise architecture departments, security organizations, platform engineering teams, quality assurance professionals, and technical leadership.

Consider a company with hundreds of developers, thousands of software changes, years of incident reports, and an extensive history of architectural decisions.

The organization may possess enormous amounts of engineering information, yet still struggle to answer a seemingly simple question:

Have we encountered something like this before, what happened, and what should that history tell us about the decision we are making today?

That is the central problem I want OME to address.

The ultimate beneficiaries are not only individual developers. They are also the people responsible for maintaining the reliability, security, continuity, and long term integrity of complex software systems.

What

Defining the Organizational Memory Engine

What exactly is the Organizational Memory Engine?

The Organizational Memory Engine is a software intelligence and engineering governance platform designed to transform accumulated technical experience into structured, retrievable, and actionable organizational knowledge.

Its purpose is to help organizations understand their software history and apply that understanding to present and future engineering decisions.

Every software organization creates a continuous stream of technical evidence: code changes, pull requests, architectural revisions, deployment outcomes, vulnerabilities, incidents, reversions, operational failures, corrective actions, and successful implementations.

Most of that evidence is fragmented across repositories, documentation systems, development tools, and the experience of individual engineers.

OME is designed to connect those fragments into a coherent organizational memory.

It combines several disciplines: information retrieval, structural software analysis, machine learning, repository topology, historical evaluation, evidence provenance, decision support, and operational governance.

But I consider the most important distinction to be this:

OME is not simply intended to remember what an organization did. It is intended to help the organization understand what it learned.

How would the completed platform work in a real engineering environment?

Imagine an engineer proposing a substantial modification to an authentication service, database architecture, or deployment pipeline.

Before that change is accepted, OME should be capable of examining the relevant engineering history.

It would identify comparable previous changes, analyze structural similarities, examine known outcomes, evaluate relevant dependencies and repository relationships, and present the available evidence in a form that supports a reasoned decision.

It might discover that similar changes previously caused incidents. It might find that earlier implementations succeeded only when particular safeguards were present.

It might also conclude that the available historical evidence is insufficient to support a confident recommendation.

That last possibility is essential.

I do not want a system that feels obligated to produce certainty when the underlying evidence does not justify it.

The intended outcome is an informed assessment explaining the relevant precedents, risk indicators, uncertainties, and limitations.

Beyond individual code changes, the wider platform is designed to support controlled evaluation, release qualification, deployment evidence, operational reconciliation, recovery oversight, and historical accountability.

The goal is to connect engineering knowledge with the governance of consequential technical actions.

Why

The Fundamental Problem

Why did you decide this kind of software needed to exist?

Because organizations repeatedly lose knowledge that they have already paid to acquire.

An incident occurs. Engineers investigate it, identify the cause, implement a correction, and document the result.

Months or years later, another team introduces a similar problem.

The original engineers may have moved to another project or left the organization. The relevant knowledge exists somewhere, but it is no longer readily accessible in a form that can inform the new decision.

The organization repeats the learning process.

That represents more than an operational inefficiency. It is a failure of institutional continuity.

Traditional software development tools are very effective at storing information. They are less effective at transforming that information into persistent, context aware organizational knowledge.

A repository can tell you what changed. A ticket can tell you what someone reported. An incident log can describe what went wrong.

But these systems do not automatically provide a coherent understanding of how the past should influence a new engineering decision.

My ambition is to reduce the distance between an organization's accumulated experience and its ability to use that experience intelligently.

Why is organizational memory so important as software systems become more complex?

Because complexity accumulates faster than individual understanding.

Modern software systems may contain thousands of components, multiple development teams, interconnected services, and operational dependencies that are difficult for any one person to comprehend fully.

Human expertise remains indispensable, but it is distributed, incomplete, and vulnerable to organizational change.

A serious engineering organization should not depend entirely on whether the right individual happens to remember a previous problem.

Its institutional knowledge should survive personnel changes, reorganizations, technological migrations, and years of accumulated development.

Ultimately, I want engineering organizations to develop the capacity to retain and critically reassess their own technical experience.

That is the deeper meaning of organizational memory.

Where

Deployment, Independence, and Organizational Control

Where do you envision the Organizational Memory Engine operating?

My long term vision is for OME to operate within professionally managed software engineering environments, where organizations can retain appropriate control over their source code, engineering history, security policies, and operational evidence.

That could include private infrastructure, controlled enterprise environments, or suitably secured cloud deployments, depending on the eventual product architecture and customer requirements.

I do not want the product's identity or engineering integrity to depend on one particular hosting provider.

OME should remain a standalone intellectual property asset, with clearly defined interfaces and a controlled execution boundary.

The underlying principle is straightforward: an organization should understand what the system processes, what evidence it retains, and what authority it has been granted.

Would OME become dependent on external AI providers or development platforms?

I distinguish between using external technology and surrendering architectural control to it.

An effective product may use carefully evaluated external libraries, models, or services where they provide measurable value.

But its critical governance principles should remain understandable and independently enforceable.

For example, an intelligence component may produce an assessment. That assessment must not automatically become permission to execute a production operation.

Those are fundamentally different responsibilities.

My architecture separates analysis, evidence, review, authorization, and execution precisely because those boundaries matter.

The platform should be designed around accountable decisions, not uncontrolled automation.

When

The Engineering Lifecycle and the Future

At what point should an organization use OME?

Ideally, throughout the software engineering lifecycle.

Its role begins when a change is being considered, not merely after something has gone wrong.

Historical precedents can inform architecture reviews, code reviews, risk assessments, release qualification, and decisions about whether an experimental model or proposed modification has sufficient evidence behind it.

Later in the lifecycle, operational records can help reconstruct what was approved, what was actually deployed, what happened afterward, and whether recovery measures remain valid.

My ultimate objective is continuity across those stages.

The same organizational knowledge system should help teams understand the reasoning behind previous decisions and preserve the consequences of those decisions for future evaluation.

When do you consider a product like OME genuinely ready for the market?

Not simply when the code compiles, a demonstration works, or a large number of automated tests pass.

Those achievements are necessary, but they are not sufficient.

A commercially credible engineering platform needs reproducible validation, an adequately defined security model, dependable data handling, clear operational boundaries, documented limitations, and a demonstrable customer problem.

Any claim about predictive accuracy or operational improvement must be supported by appropriate evidence, preferably including prospective evaluation under realistic conditions.

I do not believe in announcing a release date first and then adjusting the definition of readiness to meet it.

The product should satisfy its acceptance criteria before the acceptance decision is made.

That principle governs my development philosophy.

How

Intelligence, Evidence, and Human Authority

How does OME differ from conventional code analysis and AI development assistants?

Conventional static analysis is generally concerned with identifiable properties of code. AI development assistants frequently focus on generating, explaining, or modifying code.

Those are useful capabilities, but OME addresses a different primary question.

It asks what an organization can reasonably learn from its own engineering history, and what evidence should influence a consequential technical decision.

Its intended differentiation is the combination of historical precedent retrieval, structural risk analysis, repository relationships, temporal evaluation, evidence governance, and controlled operational decision making.

It is not enough to generate a recommendation. I want to know what supports that recommendation, what limitations apply, and whether the evidence actually justifies the next action.

Of course, differentiation must ultimately be demonstrated against existing products, not merely asserted through architectural descriptions.

Do you intend OME to replace engineering judgment?

No.

I want it to strengthen engineering judgment.

There is a dangerous tendency to confuse computational sophistication with reliable authority.

A system may analyze millions of records and still reach the wrong conclusion because its data are incomplete, its assumptions are invalid, or the environment has changed.

That is why OME's architecture deliberately distinguishes evidence from permission.

An assessment is not an authorization. A successful test is not a production deployment. A cryptographically valid record is not automatically proof that every historical claim inside it is true.

Human accountability and clearly defined governance remain essential.

The objective is not to eliminate responsible decision makers. It is to give them a better foundation for their decisions.

How important is scientific integrity to the platform?

It is central.

If a system evaluates historical software changes, it must confront the possibility of hindsight bias, incomplete data, contaminated training sets, misleading correlations, and results that fail to generalize.

A model that appears effective when tested on information it could not have known at the time is not demonstrating the predictive capability it claims.

That is why I consider temporal separation, recorded evidence, reproducibility, uncertainty, and independent validation fundamental requirements.

I want OME to be able to distinguish among what is established, what is probable, what is unknown, and what remains unproven.

For me, scientific integrity is not a decorative feature of the documentation.

It is part of the product's identity.

Future vision

The ultimate objective

What is your ultimate ambition for the Organizational Memory Engine?

My ultimate ambition is to create an enduring engineering intelligence platform that allows organizations to preserve, interrogate, and use their accumulated technical knowledge with a degree of rigor that conventional information storage does not provide.

I envision a system that can follow an organization through years of software evolution.

A system that remembers architectural decisions, recognizes recurring failure patterns, identifies relevant precedents, helps evaluate risks, and preserves a defensible record of how important technical decisions were reached.

I want it to support organizations as they become more complex without forcing them to repeatedly rediscover their own history.

In a mature implementation, organizational memory should become a continuous source of engineering insight rather than a passive archive that is consulted only after something fails.

I also want the platform to remain intellectually honest. Where the evidence is insufficient, it should be explicit about that insufficiency.

The goal is not artificial certainty.

It is better engineering intelligence.

What is the commercial ambition behind OME?

I am developing OME as a distinct proprietary intellectual property asset with its own technical identity, documentation, validation history, and potential commercial value.

I believe organizations may be willing to invest in technology that can meaningfully improve engineering continuity, decision quality, and operational accountability.

But commercial value has to be established through genuine demand, credible differentiation, technical due diligence, and measurable usefulness.

The asset could ultimately be commercialized through an appropriate proprietary transaction, licensing arrangement, or acquisition, depending on the rights and terms involved.

I am not interested in presenting speculative revenue figures as achievements.

My priority is to develop something technically defensible and commercially meaningful, with its value supported by evidence rather than expectations.

If the Organizational Memory Engine succeeds, what would success look like to you?

Success would mean that an organization no longer treats its engineering history as a disconnected collection of repositories, reports, and forgotten decisions.

Instead, it possesses a coherent means of learning from that history.

Engineers should be able to retrieve relevant experience when they need it. Technical leaders should be able to understand the evidence behind significant decisions. Organizations should be able to evaluate whether their risk assessments are genuinely useful, rather than simply persuasive.

And when something goes wrong, the organization should become better equipped to understand what happened, preserve that knowledge, and avoid repeating preventable mistakes.

Such outcomes would have to be demonstrated through real evaluation, not assumed merely because the software exists.

But that is the standard I am working toward.

Finally, if you had to summarize your vision for OME in one statement, what would you say?

“Software organizations should not be condemned to repeatedly relearn what their own history has already taught them. My vision for the Organizational Memory Engine is to transform that history into durable, evidence based engineering intelligence — intelligence that informs decisions, preserves accountability, and enables organizations to learn continuously without surrendering control over their systems.”
